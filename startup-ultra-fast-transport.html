500 Queens / Ultra Fast Transport

Ultra Fast Transport

Judge fast transport by complete journeys, access and dependable operation rather than headline speed.

The need worth working on.

Ultra Fast Transport is a broad ambition whose value depends on where people start, where they need to go and what happens at each transfer. A fast vehicle can still produce a slow or inaccessible journey. Women travelling with children, equipment, mobility aids or after dark may identify priorities that a speed-focused model misses.

What the enterprise could offer.

Develop a journey-planning and service-comparison tool for one real mobility need. Compare timetable changes, better transfers, existing vehicles and speculative high-speed options using the same door-to-door measure. The first enterprise could coordinate services or improve access while researchers investigate more ambitious infrastructure separately.

People to build with

  • Passengers and freight customers
  • Transport operators
  • Regional mobility planners

One journey from door to door

01

Recruit varied users to describe a recurring journey, including waiting, transfers, luggage and care responsibilities.

02

Map current options and measure the complete journey under representative conditions.

03

Compare practical service changes with any proposed fast-transport architecture using explicit assumptions.

04

Test an information or coordination improvement with willing operators and user feedback.

The first result

A journey evidence map, service comparison and a practical improvement brief.

What needs to be demonstrated.

Practical pilot

The starting evidence

The catalogue's fast-transport ambition does not identify a validated vehicle, route, operating authority or construction programme.

The open question

Which part of the journey creates the greatest burden and whether speed is the most useful improvement.

The next measurement

Door-to-door time, missed connections, access barriers, reliability and user-defined comfort.

Build the means to deliver.

Useful capabilities and resources

  • User and operator participation
  • Verified service information
  • Accessible journey analysis

A possible business model

Journey planning, operator coordination or access assessment may support an early service. New transport infrastructure has its own engineering and demand case.

A leadership decision to practise

Choose whose journey defines success and ensure quieter or less frequent users influence the design.

People, consequences and decisions.

  • Average travel time can hide difficult transfers.
  • A model may ignore disrupted services.
  • A speed claim can distract from affordability and access.

If equality were being designed now...

  • What would make this journey usable for women with different bodies, work hours and caring responsibilities?
  • Would faster travel give people more control over time, or simply extend expected working hours?

Begin with women's own experiences across bodies, ages, cultures and places. Invite disagreement and choose a practical change together.
