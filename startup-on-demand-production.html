500 Queens / On Demand Production

On Demand Production

Make a useful product when it is needed, with quality, repair and local skills built into the service.

The need worth working on.

On-demand production can reduce waiting or excess stock, but it also requires reliable design files, materials, equipment, quality checks and a customer who needs the result. A small workshop should not promise to manufacture anything. The strongest starting point is a repeatable product family or repair component it can make well.

What the enterprise could offer.

Explore a local making service for a non-critical item such as a storage fitting, display component or workshop aid. Compare producing locally with buying and holding stock. Women new to technical work should have access to suitable tools, paid learning and genuine design and business decisions, not only administrative tasks.

People to build with

  • Local businesses and community facilities
  • Repairers and makers
  • Customers needing small production runs

One product from request to accepted delivery

01

Choose a non-critical product with a willing customer and a measurable acceptance specification.

02

Confirm design rights, materials, equipment capability and the quality checks required.

03

Produce a small authorised trial through a suitable workshop and record all setup, finishing and inspection time.

04

Ask the customer to test fit and use, then compare the complete service with existing supply.

The first result

A tested product, repeatable job record and a realistic quotation method.

What needs to be demonstrated.

Practical pilot

The starting evidence

The archive proposes on-demand production. It does not establish a universal manufacturing system or certified production capability.

The open question

Which product family creates enough local value to cover setup, quality control and support.

The next measurement

Accepted first-pass output, turnaround time, rework, material use and complete job cost.

Build the means to deliver.

Useful capabilities and resources

  • Suitable workshop and trained operators
  • Customer specification
  • Clear design rights and quality checks

A possible business model

A per-job making and repair service can be tested with actual orders. Price design and setup work transparently rather than hiding it in unpaid labour.

A leadership decision to practise

Set the boundary of what the workshop can reliably make and create a referral path for critical or specialist work.

People, consequences and decisions.

  • A cheap prototype may hide expensive finishing.
  • Unclear design rights can prevent reuse.
  • Poor quality control can produce parts that fit visually but fail in use.

If equality were being designed now...

  • Can women with varied experience use the tools and design files, with paid time to learn the production process?
  • Who owns the design, sets the price and decides how the value of a locally made part is shared?

Begin with women's own experiences across bodies, ages, cultures and places. Invite disagreement and choose a practical change together.
